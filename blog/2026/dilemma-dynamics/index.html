Dilemma Dynamics: when better decisions fail to sustain cooperation

October 23, 2026• Alisa Sheinkman

2026   ·   game-theory   llm   nemotron     ·   research  

Exploring AI strategies, incentives and cooperation.

A model can become better at exploiting a predictable opponent while becoming worse at sustaining reciprocity. That tension is the central result of Dilemma Dynamics, an interactive laboratory for AI decision-making in repeated games. The final evaluation completed on 25 October 2026. All 7,104 episodes are available to replay, with exact scores and a reproducible public analysis.

The experiment

In a Prisoner’s Dilemma, mutual cooperation earns three points each. Defection against cooperation earns five for the defector and zero for the cooperator; mutual defection earns one each. Players maximise their own cumulative points. Another encounter can make cooperation valuable, because today’s choice changes how a responsive opponent behaves tomorrow.

The experiment compares NVIDIA Nemotron Nano and Super under two procedures. Direct makes one thinking-off action request. Draft first generates a thinking-on draft capped at 1,024 tokens, then makes a separate thinking-off action request capped at 128 tokens and conditioned on that draft. Both run at temperatures 0 and 1, with top-p 1. Draft adds compute, an extra request and conditioning together; its effects concern that complete bounded procedure.

Both temperatures were interleaved in one campaign on 24–25 October, with 48 matched environment blocks and 3,552 episodes per temperature. The benchmark contains 6,144 episodes against disclosed Always Cooperate, Always Defect, Tit-for-Tat and Grim policies. Another 960 autonomous dyads cover all ten model/procedure pairings, including self-play. Four balanced strata vary the neutral X/Y action mapping and display seat. A frozen rule selected the block count from engineering cost and latency before scientific outcomes.

Drafting improves the average, with a consistent opponent reversal

Against a disclosed rule, exact dynamic programming identifies the best achievable expected own payoff. Decision loss measures the expected payoff given up by each action, assuming optimal play afterward, and normalises for the horizon. Lower loss is better. Cooperation has a separate interpretation: it can preserve reciprocity, but sacrifices points against a partner who always cooperates.

Each arm/temperature mean has 768 benchmark episodes. All four planned intervals lie below zero. They use 50,000 stratified whole-block bootstrap draws and 98.75% marginal intervals for an approximate four-comparison Bonferroni treatment; coverage is not guaranteed. Relative loss reductions range from 16.8% to 37.5%.

The opponent breakdown changes the story. Drafting increases average loss against Tit-for-Tat in all four comparisons, with effects from +0.1717 to +0.3225. It improves all four means against Always Cooperate and Grim. Much of the gain comes from recognising simple exploitation opportunities. Against TFT, errors remain in regimes that reward alternation and in regimes that reward sustained cooperation.

Better benchmark performance does not ensure cooperative interaction

Every autonomous pairing has 48 dyads and 376 realised rounds per temperature. Across pairings, mutual cooperation occurs in 330/3,760 rounds (8.8%) at zero and 353/3,760 (9.4%) at one. These rounds share histories and are statistically dependent. Changing both self-play agents from Direct to Draft lowers mutual cooperation and joint-return point estimates for each model at each temperature.

Super at zero gives the clearest example. Direct self-play has 124/376 CC rounds, whereas Draft has 3/376. Mean normalised joint return falls from 3.2063 to 1.8667; the paired difference is −1.3396, with exploratory nominal 95% interval [−1.8375, −0.9042]. Direct never starts with mutual cooperation but later returns to it in 82/218 eligible transitions. Draft never returns to CC in 325 opportunities. The initial choice and the capacity to repair a relationship are different behaviours.

Temperature, labels and planning expose remaining weaknesses

The six planned secondary temperature comparisons have nominal 95% intervals without multiplicity correction. Super Direct improves at zero, Super Draft changes little, Nano Draft worsens, and Nano Direct changes little. Both models’ drafting advantages narrow at zero. There is no uniform preferred temperature across the procedures.

Two exploratory diagnostics are especially revealing:

  • Presentation sensitivity: at zero, Nano’s drafting effect reverses sign between X=cooperate and X=defect in both seats. Different stopping draws also differ across strata, so a fully crossed seed/mapping experiment would be needed to isolate the label effect.
  • Limited sustained planning: against the committed TFT/Grim rules with ten announced rounds, nine C moves followed by D is optimal and earns 32 points. Only one of 768 episodes follows that path. Super Draft often plays the useful two-round TFT endgame while losing payoff earlier.

These patterns motivate hypotheses about shallow contingent planning, defensive play that blocks repair, presentation habits, and interactions between sampling and truncation. They remain hypotheses. Future tests could vary planning depth, introduce controlled forgiving partners, cross every seed with every mapping, and randomise draft caps while holding compute budgets comparable. The final report develops these proposals alongside the related literature and Rasmusen’s repeated-game foundations.

Infrastructure, recovery and reproducibility

The local CPU coordinator used remote Nebius Token Factory inference, native Inspect AI samples/model events/scorers, and local MLflow Skinny tracking in SQLite. Both integrations were used during execution. The canonical ledger preserves requests, sealed choices, joint reveals, accounting and source hashes. A provider-overload interruption at 3,406 episodes led to a separately approved bounded transport amendment; all completed observations and the original deadline were retained. The scientific stage finished in 16.16 hours, including recovery.

There were 62,832 successful requests plus 20 additional transmissions. Recorded final usage is estimated at $17.1520, with $0.6349 for engineering and $0.32479584 still retained as uncertain maximum billing exposure. These are usage estimates and exposure bounds, not provider invoices. Publication with that uncertainty disclosed was explicitly approved under the existing additional $40 cap; no reservation was cleared. Draft costs approximately 10–12 times Direct within the same model across both tracks, and 25–56% of successful drafts reach their token cap.

This is the sole public final evaluation. Earlier outcomes informed the design but are excluded. The package reproduces scores, planned comparisons and exploratory diagnostics from saved actions, without further inference. Public hashes check integrity; privately retained native logs support execution review. Hosted responses are not guaranteed byte-identical, including at temperature zero.
